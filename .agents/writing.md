# Scientific writing

Use this module for papers, technical notes, captions, literature summaries, and research claims.

The source manuscripts show a consistent style at several scales. Treat the patterns below as defaults, not quotas.

## Word and sentence scale

- Use ordinary technical vocabulary rather than literary or promotional language.
- Prefer established terms over stylistic synonyms.
- Use first-person plural mainly for scientific actions: "we define", "we derive", "we compute", "we compare", "we show".
- Let the scientific object be the grammatical subject when possible: "The smallest eigenvalue measures...", "The approximation holds...", "The structural network gives...".
- Prefer medium-length sentences that carry one main relation. Longer sentences are useful when a causal, conditional, or contrastive relation belongs together.
- Prefer declarative scientific statements to rhetorical questions. In introductions, state the problem and result directly rather than asking a question and immediately answering it.
- Avoid colon-heavy prose. Use a colon only when it performs a clear structural role, such as introducing a definition, list, or genuinely dependent clause.
- Do not make the prose artificially choppy.
- Do not imitate informal chat spelling, typos, or unfinished phrasing.

Natural technical verbs include:
- consider;
- define;
- introduce;
- derive;
- obtain;
- compute;
- analyze;
- compare;
- apply;
- show;
- find.

Avoid generic assistant or promotional phrasing such as:
- delve into;
- leverage;
- unlock;
- shed light on;
- pave the way;
- key takeaway;
- powerful insight;
- remarkable;
- compelling.

Use "important", "crucial", "novel", or "interesting" only when the sentence explains the concrete reason.

## Sentence function

A sentence should normally do at least one scientific job:

- define an object;
- state a fact or result;
- give a reason;
- describe a mechanism;
- specify an assumption or limitation;
- connect two scientific steps.

Delete sentences whose main role is to announce the structure, correct an imagined misunderstanding, or restate the same point from the opposite direction.

Prefer:

> The smallest eigenvalue measures the residual incompatibility of the connection.

over:

> It is important to note that this should not be interpreted as a local mismatch; rather, it provides a measure of the global residual incompatibility.

Use contrast only when the contrast carries scientific information.

## Paragraph scale

Paragraphs in the source work are usually substantive rather than one- or two-sentence fragments.

A common paragraph shape is:

1. state the object, question, or result;
2. introduce the mechanism, definition, or evidence;
3. develop the mathematical or numerical consequence;
4. end with the interpretation, scope, or link to the next step.

Do not force all four steps when fewer are sufficient.

Keep one main scientific move per paragraph. If a paragraph contains two independent claims, split it. If two short paragraphs perform one continuous move, combine them.

The first sentence should usually contain scientific content immediately. Avoid generic scene-setting.

The final sentence should add consequence or scope when useful; do not append a generic summary sentence to every paragraph.

## Section scale

Use descriptive section and subsection titles. Prefer the mechanism, object, or result itself over catchy labels or questions.

Use sentence case for paper titles, section titles, subsection titles, and descriptive caption openings. Do not use headline/title case merely for emphasis.

Prefer a direct single title over a title + subtitle construction. Use a colon only when the scientific content genuinely needs two levels.

A results subsection should normally answer one identifiable scientific question.

A common progression is:

baseline object -> new quantity/model -> derivation -> consequence -> numerical or empirical validation -> harder/general case.

Organize by logical dependence, not by the chronology of what was tried.

Methods can appear after the conceptual/results story when the journal format and project support that structure.

Avoid roadmap paragraphs such as "The paper is organized as follows" unless the document is unusually complex or the venue expects one.

## Paper scale

A common scientific path is:

problem -> precise gap -> object/model -> result -> interpretation -> evidence.

Do not force this structure when the project already has a clearer one.

### Title

Prefer a descriptive title built from the scientific object/mechanism and the concrete phenomenon or result.

Observed forms include:
- mechanism/object + what it captures;
- structural quantity + what it predicts;
- measured trajectory + what it reveals.

Avoid generic or promotional titles.

### Abstract

The source abstracts are compact and result-bearing, typically several substantive sentences rather than many short fragments.

Prefer:

1. phenomenon or problem;
2. precise unresolved issue;
3. object, method, or framework introduced;
4. main analytical or empirical result;
5. relevant validation or scope;
6. concrete closing consequence.

The actual scientific object and result should appear in the abstract. Do not spend most of it motivating the field.

### Introduction

Narrow progressively:

1. establish the phenomenon;
2. identify the specific limitation of current descriptions;
3. explain why that limitation matters here;
4. introduce the proposed object or model;
5. state the main result and scope.

Use literature to define the problem and gap, not as a catalogue.

### Results

Develop the argument in the order needed to understand the result.

Introduce the simplest object first, then the new quantity or mechanism, then the derived consequence, then the numerical/empirical check.

Do not preserve the order in which the research happened if it weakens the scientific story.

### Discussion

Begin with the concrete finding.

Then:
- explain the mechanism;
- separate nearby but non-equivalent effects;
- state the scope of the inference;
- state relevant limitations;
- discuss extensions only after the result is clear.

## Equations in prose

Equations are part of the argument.

Before a displayed equation, state what is being defined, compared, or derived.

After it:
- define new symbols close to first use;
- state the mathematical or physical meaning;
- connect it to the next step.

Do not repeat an equation in words unless the prose adds interpretation.

Prefer one conceptual step per display when practical.

## Figures and captions

Every figure should answer a scientific question.

In the surrounding text:
- state what the figure tests;
- identify the relevant trend, threshold, comparison, or failure;
- connect it to the analytical or conceptual claim.

Open captions directly with the scientific object/result rather than "This figure shows..." or "Here we plot...".

The source captions commonly follow this order:

1. short descriptive statement of the figure's object or result;
2. panel-by-panel description when needed;
3. meaning of lines, markers, colors, bands, or thresholds;
4. sample size, averaging, model, or parameter details required for interpretation.

Do not use the caption to repeat the discussion.

## Literature and claims

- Prefer primary sources.
- Cite the closest relevant prior work.
- State what previous work established.
- Distinguish known results, reformulations, extensions, new derivations, and new observations.
- Do not infer novelty from unfamiliar notation.
- Do not make "first", "never", or equivalent priority claims without checking.
- Keep citation groups small enough that their role is clear.
- Keep exact results, approximations, numerical observations, interpretations, and hypotheses visibly distinct.

Prefer:

> We extend X by allowing Y, which leads to Z.

over a broad priority claim when the literature does not require one.

## Editing existing text

Preserve a functioning argument.

When polishing:

1. remove repetition and vague framing;
2. make the scientific distinction precise;
3. shorten unnecessary setup;
4. connect equations and figures to the argument;
5. add material only when it fixes a real gap.

Do not rewrite a paragraph merely to make it sound different.
