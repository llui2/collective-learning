\section{Task allocation}

We tested whether division of labor could emerge among the coupled
learners of Arola-Fern\'andez and Lacasa \cite{arola2024}.
Each network learns two tasks, with allocation $a_i$ controlling its
training effort:
\begin{equation}
L_i = a_i L_A(\theta_i) + (1-a_i)L_B(\theta_i)
+ \frac{\gamma}{2}\|\theta_i\|^2,
\qquad
\theta_i^{t+1} = \theta_i^t - \eta\nabla L_i
+ \frac{\eta\sigma}{N}\sum_j A_{ij}(\theta_j^t-\theta_i^t).
\label{eq:learning}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma$ controls parameter exchange. Every learner is evaluated
on both tasks, regardless of its allocation.

Assigning complementary specialists ($a_i=0$ or $1$) gives a
\emph{transient learning-speed advantage} over generalists
($a_i=1/2$) when coupling is sufficient (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a).
Without coupling, specialists cannot learn their unassigned task.

We then let individual allocations mutate, retaining a mutation
when a short validation rollout improved collective loss.
This rule did not discover the beneficial division of labor:
the initially faster adaptation later fell behind generalists
(Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b).

\begin{figure}[htb!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/arola.pdf}
\caption{Learning advantage relative to generalists, averaged over
12 paired runs at $\sigma=1$. Positive values indicate lower
individual test loss. (a) Imposed specialization.
(b) Assigned specialists, selected mutations and neutral drift
in the adaptive experiment.}
\label{fig:results}
\end{figure}

The useful allocation is \emph{complementary}, whereas the rule
evaluates isolated changes using short-term, global validation
loss. This mismatch may prevent coordinated specialization;
the experiment does not establish it as the cause of failure.
