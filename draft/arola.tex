\section*{Where we started}

The idea in Arola-Fern\'andez and Lacasa \cite{arola2024} is simple:
several neural networks learn from their own data, but also exchange
information by pulling their parameters toward one another. Each
learner still has to learn; coupling only changes how information
can move through the population. Schematically,
\begin{equation}
\theta_i(t+1)=\theta_i(t)-\eta\nabla_{\theta_i}L_i
+\frac{\eta\sigma}{N}\sum_j A_{ij}(\theta_j-\theta_i).
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma$ controls the strength of that exchange. The paper
also derives a simpler description of deep linear networks,
replacing their many parameters by effective variables.

We implemented the effective-theory baseline and checked its
dependence on coupling and network depth (Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline}).
We also ran a short, three-seed MNIST check; those neural runs
were \emph{not} a full reproduction of the published protocol.
The new question below was an extension, not a test of whether
the original theory was correct.

\section*{The question we wanted to answer}

What if learners do \emph{not} spend equal effort on every task?
Could one network focus on task A and another on task B, then
share what they learn through coupling? Would each network
learn both tasks faster than if everyone studied everything?

To test the idea, we used four small deep-linear networks and
two simple tasks that every network could, in principle, learn.
We kept their training effort, examples, and initial weights
matched across conditions. We always evaluated \emph{every
individual network on both tasks}: a better group prediction
alone would not answer our question.

\section*{First test: assign specialization by hand}

We first compared two arrangements. In one, every network
trained equally on both tasks. In the other, half trained
only on A and half only on B. This division of labor was
\emph{imposed from the beginning}; the networks did not choose it.

At intermediate coupling, the specialists learned both tasks
faster. For $\sigma=1$, specialists reached mean individual
test loss $0.2$ in a median of $150$ updates, compared with
$185$ for generalists; they were faster in 11 of 12 paired runs.
Without coupling, they never reached that threshold within
600 updates: knowing only one's own task was not enough.
With weak coupling, the information arrived too slowly and
specialization was usually worse.

So there \emph{was} a benefit to dividing the work, but only
when networks could exchange what they learned effectively.

\section*{Second test: let specialization evolve}

We then asked whether a division of labor would develop
by itself. We started with nearly equal task allocations,
occasionally changed one learner's allocation, and kept
the change if a short trial reduced the population's
average validation loss. We also tested fixed generalists,
fixed specialists, unchanged near-generalists, and random
allocation drift.

The selected allocations \emph{did not} reproduce the speedup
of the hand-assigned specialists (Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}).
They became different from one another, but random drift
produced at least as much allocation differentiation.
In this experiment, becoming different was not the same
as finding a useful division of labor.

\section*{What was the problem?}

The two tests answered different questions. The first showed that
\emph{a good division of labor exists}. The second tried to find
it by changing just one learner at a time and rewarding
\emph{immediate} improvements in population loss.

But the useful arrangement asks learners to take
\emph{complementary} roles. A change that helps only after
someone else changes might not be selected on its own.
Our rule also used global validation data and extra
counterfactual training: it was not an individual,
ecologically grounded evolutionary mechanism.
These are plausible reasons for the negative result,
\emph{not} a demonstrated explanation of it.

\section*{Takeaway}

We found that coupling can make imposed specialization useful,
but we did \emph{not} show how a population discovers it.
That is a limitation of our proposed adaptive extension,
not a failure of Arola's original model.

We have kept the baseline and negative results as a record.
The main project now studies mixture-of-experts architectures,
where a learned router and the experts change each other's
training conditions directly.
