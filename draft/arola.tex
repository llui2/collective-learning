\section{Task allocation}

We tested whether a useful division of labor could emerge in
coupled learners \cite{arola2024}. Each network allocates a fraction
$a_i$ of its effort to task $A$ and the rest to $B$, while
parameter diffusion exchanges what the networks learn:
\begin{align}
L_i &= a_i L_A(\theta_i) + (1-a_i) L_B(\theta_i)
+ \frac{\gamma}{2}\|\theta_i\|^2,\\
\theta_i^{t+1} &= \theta_i^t - \eta\nabla_{\theta_i}L_i
+ \frac{\eta\sigma}{N}\sum_j A_{ij}(\theta_j^t-\theta_i^t).
\end{align}
Here $\sigma$ controls coupling. Every network is tested on
both tasks, regardless of its allocation.

\emph{Imposed specialization works temporarily.}
Assigning complementary tasks to different learners improves
early learning when coupling transfers their knowledge.

\emph{Adaptive specialization does not emerge.}
When individual allocations mutate and are selected by
short-term collective validation loss, they become unequal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a), but this differentiation produces
no sustained learning advantage (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}b).
Random drift gives a similar degree of differentiation.

\begin{figure}[htb!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\linewidth]{figures/arola.pdf}
\caption{Allocation change does not imply useful specialization.
(a) Distance of allocations from equal effort; $P=0$ for
generalists and $P=1$ for fully polarized learners.
(b) Reduction in individual test loss relative to generalists;
positive values indicate an advantage. Mean trajectories and
standard-deviation bands over 12 paired runs at $\sigma=1$.}
\label{fig:results}
\end{figure}

The rule selects one learner's mutation at a time, although
complementary specialization may require coordinated changes.
This offers a possible explanation for the failure, not a
demonstrated one. The result concerns our adaptation rule,
not the original coupling model.
