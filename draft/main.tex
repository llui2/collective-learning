\title{Spontaneous differentiation in collective learning}
\maketitle

\section{Literature}

Collective learning can be modeled as a population of neural networks whose parameters evolve under local learning and diffusive coupling. Arola-Fernández and Lacasa introduced a minimal dynamics of this form and derived an effective theory in which coupling competes with heterogeneity generated by the private data available to each learner \cite{arola2024}. The resulting coarse-grained description is a disordered Ginzburg--Landau model and exhibits a coupling-driven collective learning phase. In this construction, however, the heterogeneity between learners is imposed by the data partition and remains fixed during learning.

Evolutionary dynamics provides a different mechanism for heterogeneity. Selection and mutation can change the distribution of heritable strategies according to their performance in the current population \cite{page2002}. When the fitness of a strategy depends on the strategies already present, a homogeneous population need not remain stable. This suggests a direct extension of collective learning in which the source of heterogeneity is itself dynamical.

We consider a population of coupled neural learners exposed to the same data distribution. Each learner has ordinary neural parameters, which change rapidly by backpropagation, and a slow learning strategy that changes through selection and mutation. The slow variable does not prescribe a private dataset. Instead, it changes how a common stream of examples contributes to the local gradient. The main problem is then whether coupling, which tends to homogenize neural parameters, can coexist with or suppress spontaneous differentiation of learning strategies.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Coupled learners in a common environment}

Consider $N$ neural networks with identical architecture. The parameters of learner $i$ are denoted by
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i
=
(\theta_i^1,\ldots,\theta_i^P),
\end{equation}
and the prediction of the network for an input $x$ is $f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)$. All learners receive the same minibatch
\begin{equation}
B_t
=
\{(x_b,y_b)\}_{b=1}^{n}
\end{equation}
drawn from a common distribution.

Without an additional source of heterogeneity, the microscopic dynamics follows the same structure as the coupled learning model of Ref.~\cite{arola2024},
\begin{equation}
\theta_i^\alpha(t+1)
=
\theta_i^\alpha(t)
-
\frac{\eta}{n}
\sum_{b=1}^{n}
\partial_{\theta_i^\alpha}
\ell\!\left(
f(x_b;\boldsymbol{\theta}_i),
y_b
\right)
+
\frac{\eta\sigma}{N}
\sum_j q_{ij}
\left(
\theta_j^\alpha-\theta_i^\alpha
\right),
\label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}
where $\eta$ is the learning rate, $\sigma$ is the coupling strength, and $q_{ij}$ specifies the interaction network. The first term performs local learning and the second tends to homogenize the parameters of connected learners.

If all networks see the same data and have the same learning rule, Eq.~\eqref{eq:baseline} contains no persistent source of functional differentiation. We therefore introduce a second dynamical variable that changes the direction of local learning without changing the available data.

\subsection{A slow learning genotype}

Each learner carries a vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf g_i
\in
\mathbb R^r,
\end{equation}
which we refer to as its learning genotype. The neural parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$ remain the fast phenotype. The genotype is not optimized by backpropagation.

Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf h_i(x)
=
\mathbf h(x;\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)
\in
\mathbb R^r
\end{equation}
denote a representation of the input generated by the current network. The genotype assigns a score
\begin{equation}
s_i(x)
=
\frac{
\mathbf g_i^{\mathsf T}
\sg[\mathbf h_i(x)]
}{T},
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $T>0$ controls the strength of the preference and $\sg[\cdot]$ denotes a stop-gradient operation. The representation can therefore change during learning, but the allocation weights are treated as fixed when differentiating the local loss with respect to $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$.

For a minibatch $B_t$, define the normalized sample weights
\begin{equation}
w_i(x_b\mid B_t)
=
n\,
\frac{
\exp[s_i(x_b)]
}{
\sum_{c=1}^{n}
\exp[s_i(x_c)]
}.
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
They satisfy
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{n}
\sum_{b=1}^{n}
w_i(x_b\mid B_t)
=
1.
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Every learner therefore receives the same total gradient budget and has access to every sample in the batch. The genotype only redistributes this finite learning effort across the examples currently available.

The neural weights then evolve according to
\begin{align}
\theta_i^\alpha(t+1)
&=
\theta_i^\alpha(t)
-
\frac{\eta}{n}
\sum_{b=1}^{n}
w_i(x_b\mid B_t)
\partial_{\theta_i^\alpha}
\ell\!\left(
f(x_b;\boldsymbol{\theta}_i),
y_b
\right)
\nonumber\\
&\quad
+
\frac{\eta\sigma}{N}
\sum_j q_{ij}
\left(
\theta_j^\alpha-\theta_i^\alpha
\right).
\label{eq:microscopic}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:microscopic} is the microscopic modification of the collective learning dynamics. Backpropagation remains unchanged apart from a multiplicative weight on each sample contribution. The genotype changes the local gradient field, while diffusive coupling still acts directly on the neural parameters.

The uniform-learning limit is recovered when $T\to\infty$. In this limit $s_i(x)\to0$ and
\begin{equation}
w_i(x_b\mid B_t)
\to
1,
\end{equation}
so Eq.~\eqref{eq:microscopic} reduces to Eq.~\eqref{eq:baseline}. Heterogeneity is therefore generated only by finite genotype-dependent preferences.

\subsection{Selection acts on the learning strategy}

The slow dynamics acts on the distribution of genotypes rather than on the neural weights. Let
\begin{equation}
\tau
=
\varepsilon t,
\qquad
0<\varepsilon\ll1,
\end{equation}
denote the evolutionary time. For a large population, let $P(\mathbf g,\tau)$ be the density of learning genotypes. A minimal mutation--selection dynamics is
\begin{equation}
\partial_\tau P
=
\left[
F(\mathbf g;P)-\overline F
\right]
P
+
\mu
\nabla_{\mathbf g}^2 P,
\label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\overline F
=
\int
F(\mathbf g;P)
P(\mathbf g,\tau)
\,\mathrm d\mathbf g.
\end{equation}
The first term amplifies learning strategies with above-average fitness and the second represents small mutations in genotype space. In a finite population, Eq.~\eqref{eq:replicator} can be implemented by mutation and fitness-dependent resampling on the slow timescale.

The fitness should measure contribution to collective performance rather than only individual accuracy. One concrete choice is the marginal contribution of learner $i$. Define the collective predictor
\begin{equation}
\overline f(x)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_i
f(x;\boldsymbol{\theta}_i)
\end{equation}
and its validation loss $L$. Let $L_{-i}$ be the same loss when learner $i$ is removed from the ensemble. Then
\begin{equation}
F_i
=
L_{-i}-L.
\label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
A learner has positive fitness when its removal worsens the collective prediction. This makes the value of a learning strategy population dependent: a strategy that produces information already supplied by many other learners can have a smaller marginal contribution than a strategy occupying a poorly represented niche.

Equations~\eqref{eq:microscopic} and \eqref{eq:replicator} define two coupled timescales,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i
\longrightarrow
F_i
\longrightarrow
P(\mathbf g)
\longrightarrow
w_i(x)
\longrightarrow
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i.
\label{eq:feedback}
\end{equation}
The neural phenotype determines fitness, selection changes the distribution of learning strategies, and the genotype distribution reshapes subsequent backpropagation.

\subsection{Coarse-grained description}

The microscopic construction suggests an effective theory in which the fixed disorder of the original collective learning problem is replaced by an adaptive field. Let $m_{i\mu}$ denote the amplitude learned by unit $i$ along a coarse-grained learning direction $\mu=1,\ldots,K$. Let
\begin{equation}
\mathbf a_i
=
(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{iK}),
\qquad
a_{i\mu}\geq0,
\qquad
\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}a_{i\mu}=1,
\label{eq:simplex}
\end{equation}
be the coarse-grained allocation generated by the genotype.

A minimal effective ansatz is
\begin{align}
\dot m_{i\mu}
&=
a_{i\mu}
R_\mu(m_{i\mu})
-
\gamma m_{i\mu}
+
\frac{\sigma}{N}
\sum_j q_{ij}
\left(
m_{j\mu}-m_{i\mu}
\right),
\label{eq:effective-m}
\\
\dot a_{i\mu}
&=
\varepsilon
a_{i\mu}
\left(
F_{i\mu}
-
\sum_\nu
a_{i\nu}F_{i\nu}
\right)
+
\varepsilon\mu
\left(
\frac{1}{K}-a_{i\mu}
\right).
\label{eq:effective-a}
\end{align}
Here $R_\mu$ denotes the effective local learning dynamics along direction $\mu$. Equation~\eqref{eq:effective-m} is not yet a derivation from Eq.~\eqref{eq:microscopic}; it isolates the structural change that the microscopic model introduces. The learning rate along each direction becomes an adaptive quantity, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective-a} lets this allocation change on a slower timescale.

The permutation-symmetric state satisfies
\begin{equation}
a_{i\mu}
=
\frac{1}{K}
\end{equation}
for all $i$ and $\mu$. Its stability is the central theoretical problem. Diffusive coupling in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective-m} suppresses differences between learners, while frequency-dependent fitness in Eq.~\eqref{eq:effective-a} can amplify differences in learning allocation.

Two order parameters separate collective learning from specialization. Define
\begin{equation}
M^2
=
\sum_{\mu=1}^{K}
\left(
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_i m_{i\mu}
\right)^2
\label{eq:collective-order}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
S
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_i
\left\|
\mathbf m_i-\overline{\mathbf m}
\right\|^2,
\qquad
\overline{\mathbf m}
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_i\mathbf m_i.
\label{eq:specialization-order}
\end{equation}
The quantity $M$ measures the magnitude of collective learning, while $S$ measures differentiation between learners. A third quantity,
\begin{equation}
G
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_i
\left\|
\mathbf a_i-\overline{\mathbf a}
\right\|^2,
\label{eq:genotype-order}
\end{equation}
measures diversity of the slow learning strategies.

The resulting problem is controlled by the competition between the coupling strength $\sigma$, the evolutionary timescale $\varepsilon$, mutation $\mu$, and the genotype preference scale $T$. The first analytical step is to determine when the symmetric state becomes unstable to perturbations in $\mathbf a_i$ and whether this instability coincides with a nonzero specialization order parameter $S$.
